### The prior sketch

A prior version of this identity existed before this session, supplied as two
raster images: a logo sheet showing a horizontal wordmark, a standalone
double-C symbol, an app icon, four colour lockups and a three-colour palette;
and a table of required deliverables. The provenance of that earlier sketch is
**not established by this record**. Whoever produced it, by what method, and on
what terms, needs to be documented separately before any assignment is relied
on. Note in particular that the earlier sheet's wordmark appears to have been
set in a typeface; if so, that typeface's licence terms need to be established,
and they do not carry over to the artwork here, which shares none of its
outlines.

### The typeface, and what it does and does not cover

**The wordmark and tagline are set in Poppins SemiBold**, designed by Indian
Type Foundry, released under the **SIL Open Font License 1.1**. The licence text
and the exact font file used are in `01-master/typeface/`, so the artwork can be
reproduced from the same source later.

What the OFL permits, and it is worth stating plainly because it is often
misread: the font may be used commercially, without payment or attribution in
the finished work, and outlines taken from it may be used in a logo and
registered as a trade mark. What the OFL restricts is redistributing or selling
the **font software** itself, and using its Reserved Font Names for modified
versions. Neither restriction touches this bundle: **the delivered artwork
contains outlines, not font data**, so nothing in the SVGs depends on the font
being installed and no font file needs to ship with any deliverable.

Two consequences to be clear-eyed about:

- **The wordmark is not exclusive.** Anyone with Poppins can set the word
  "Soccolo" and arrive at the same letterforms. Protection for the name rests on
  trade mark registration, not on the artwork being unique.
- **The symbol is separately constructed.** It was not taken from the font.
  That is useful provenance evidence, but exclusivity is not established until
  the copyright and trade-mark issues in sections 3 and 4 are resolved.

### What is not present in this artwork

- **No font data ships.** Every delivered file contains converted outlines.
- **The symbol uses no typeface.** Both symbols were constructed from ellipses
  and arcs by the generator in `01-master/source/marks.py`, dimensioned from
  measurements taken off the typeface's o but drawn independently of it.
- **No stock or third-party components.** No clip art, icon set, template,
  texture or purchased asset.
- **No tracing.** No element was drawn over an existing image.

### Reproducibility as evidence

The artwork is **parametrically generated**. `01-master/source/` contains the
complete generator; running `python3 build.py` regenerates every
generated asset in this bundle byte-for-byte from the geometry. The written
documents are authored and are preserved by the build.

This is a stronger provenance artefact than a layered vector file. A `.ai` or
`.fig` file shows the finished shapes. The generator shows the *derivation*: the
radii, the solved equations, the optical corrections and the reasons for each,
all in readable form. It demonstrates that the mark was constructed rather than
copied, which is exactly the question a similarity dispute turns on.

Keep the source. It is part of the asset, not a build artefact.

## 3. Copyright position, and a real problem with it

**Raise this with a lawyer before relying on copyright in this artwork.**

Copyright in a work generally requires a human author. Where a work is generated
by software with limited human authorial input, its copyright status is
uncertain and varies significantly between jurisdictions. In Australia, the
authorities on computer-generated works have set a meaningful bar for human
authorship. In the United States, the Copyright Office has declined
registration for material it considered generated without sufficient human
authorship, while registering works where a human's selection and arrangement
was substantial.

The practical consequences:

- **Do not assume this artwork carries enforceable copyright.** It may carry
  some, attaching to the human selection and direction recorded in section 2.
  It may carry none. That is a question for counsel on the facts recorded here.
- **Trade mark protection does not depend on authorship.** Registrability turns
  on distinctiveness and use, not on who or what drew the mark. This is the
  primary protection route for Soccolo and it is unaffected by the above.
- **Registered design protection** may also be available for specific
  applications and is likewise not authorship-dependent.
- If enforceable copyright matters to the business (for example, because an
  investor or acquirer will ask for a warranty about it), the route is to have
  a human designer make substantial, documented creative revisions to this
  artwork and record them, not to restate the provenance more favourably.

This is a statement of issues to take to a qualified adviser, not legal advice.

**Not yet in place.** Nothing in this bundle effects a transfer of rights.

Before the mark is used commercially, put in writing:

1. Confirmation from whoever produced the **prior sketch** that they assign all

terms applicable to the account and service used to produce this bundle. Current
Anthropic public materials state that customers retain rights in inputs and own
outputs under both its Commercial and Consumer Terms, subject to those terms and
applicable law: <https://www.anthropic.com/transparency/voluntary-commitments>.
The exact source product/account type (Consumer, Claude for Work, API or a
third-party platform) is not recorded in the bundle, so the exact governing
terms/version and any platform terms must still be attached before a legal
rights warranty is made. Acceptance of provider terms does not create copyright
where applicable law would not otherwise recognize it and does not clear a
trade mark.
